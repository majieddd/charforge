Related work

Characters from an image or a prompt

Image-to-3D generators. TRELLIS [@trellis] and its successor TRELLIS.2 [@trellis2] generate meshes with physically based materials from structured sparse-voxel latents (a 10243 asset in about 9.6K tokens). TRELLIS.2 conditions on DINOv3 image features [@dinov3]. Hunyuan3D 2.0 and 2.1 [@hunyuan3d2; @hunyuan3d21] pair a shape diffusion transformer with a texture painter and produce PBR materials. Both families are general-purpose: they model a character as one surface, which is the root of most of what CharForge repairs. A practitioner's survey of hosted and open generators [@cinevvaguide] recommends the same two-step workflow we use - draw a clean reference with an image model, then lift it to 3D - and notes the same weakness: generated topology lacks deformation loops at the joints. The Cinevva workbench advertises text or up to four views, rigging, animation and GLB export, but does not identify the underlying model [@cinevvatool]. AI3DGen advertises selectable-face-count OBJ exports and personal-use terms; its cited page does not document rigging or animation [@ai3dgen].

The Qwen-Image and Qwen-Image-2.0 papers describe earlier generations in the image-model family [@qwenimage; @qwenimage2]; the separate Qwen-Image 2.1 repository documents the checkpoint actually used by CharForge [@qwenimage21]. Its license permits non-commercial research and evaluation only; it requires a separate license for commercial use [@qwenimage21license].

Character-specific systems. CharacterGen [@charactergen] canonicalises a character in any pose to four A-pose views with an image-conditioned multi-view diffusion model, reconstructs it with a transformer sparse-view model, and back-projects the views into a texture with a depth test and Poisson blending [@poisson]; it rigs with AccuRIG [@accurig]. Make-A-Character [@mach] takes the opposite route: a language model parses the prompt into attributes, a portrait is generated and fitted onto a fixed, production-rigged template (MetaHuman) using 431 dense facial landmarks, hair is built as strands, and garments are matched from a library by CLIP similarity. Make-A-Character 2 [@mach2] starts from a single portrait, corrects its lighting and skin tone, and calibrates a facial skeleton for expressive animation. Template methods get rigging and faces for free but not the look of an arbitrary character; generation methods get the look but inherit the generator's topology. CharForge generates the character (like CharacterGen) and then builds the rig from the generated solid itself.

Decomposed and product-ready generators, and open peers. StdGEN [@stdgen] and StdGEN++ [@stdgenpp] generate a character as separate body, clothes and hair meshes from a canonical A-pose, the second adding a video-diffusion texture decomposition into editable layers; DreamCharacter-1 [@dreamcharacter] post-trains a 3D foundation model for characters with geometry preference optimisation, two-stage texturing and distillation; and Pixal3D [@pixal3d] conditions the TRELLIS.2 backbone by back-projecting image features along camera rays, so the shape is aligned pixel for pixel with the input view. Code is released for StdGEN and Pixal3D (CUDA; Pixal3D also through a third-party Apple Silicon port) and, as far as we could find, not for DreamCharacter-1 [E153]. Two open projects do the whole task on one machine: OpenMeshy [@openmeshy] chains TRELLIS.2, Hunyuan3D and Pixal3D backends with retopology, a pass that copies the source picture's pixels onto the surface, SkinTokens rigging and Unity Humanoid export, and writes a record of the licences behind each output; reference-asset-compiler [@refassetcompiler] gates every stage on recorded evidence, including a five-pose deformation suite for the rig, and never retries a crashed model run. The pages we read describe neither foot planting, interpenetration nor measured motion quality - the layers this paper adds. They had two things CharForge lacked, and took from them: a licence record with each package (PROVENANCE.json) and an extreme-pose check on skinning ([#sec-eval-stress]).

Commercial generators. Meshy's API generates a mesh and texture from one picture, then optionally remeshes it to quad-dominant or triangle topology at a target count (100-300,000, default 30,000) and textures it at 2K, 4K or 8K with optional metallic, roughness and normal maps, delighting the base colour by default; its rigging step takes a textured humanoid facing +Z and returns a rigged model with walking and running clips [@meshyapi]. Tripo adapts its face count to the shape (about 1.4 million faces, two million at its detailed setting), offers quad output up to 150,000 faces, a 'smart low-poly' topology of 500-20,000 faces, and texture tiers up to 8K [@tripoapi]. Neither documents how its rig is weighted or how its animations are cleaned. Against them [E162], CharForge's surface density is comparable (one million faces), but it ends in triangles, not quads. Its 4096-pixel texture holds about one megapixel of real detail, projected from an 832 x 1216 picture and 1024-pixel side and back views. Each clip carries the corrections we add to keep limbs out of the body. The roadmap takes these one lane each ([#sec-roadmap]).

Repair and representation quality. ManifoldPlus [@manifoldplus] turns triangle soups into watertight manifolds while optimizing the result back toward the input; Metro [@metro] measures simplified-surface error by sampling the source and output surfaces. These works motivate two separate checks in [E116] and [E118]: topology repair success and geometric fidelity. They do not supply a CharForge-specific tolerance. Semantic-segmentation confidence is also not automatically correctness: Wang et al. [@segcal] and de Jorge et al. [@segrel] study calibration, reliability, and shift-related failure, motivating a manually labeled, held-out calibration set before multi-view part probabilities are trusted [E117]. Neither gives CharForge-specific thresholds.

Characters from drawings. Animated Drawings [@animateddrawings] rigs a child's drawing in 2D. DrawingSpinUp [@drawingspinup] lifts a drawing to 3D, removes and later restores its view-dependent contour lines, thins single-line limbs with a skeleton-guided deformation, rigs with Mixamo's auto-rigger from eight front-view keypoints and assigns each vertex to its nearest bone; its authors report surface adhesion where the drawing has self-occlusion, the same failure our arm cut addresses. Akman et al. [@akman2020] go from stick figures to 3D bodies with a variational autoencoder and animate by interpolating in its latent space.

Unified models. PixelUMM [@pixelumm] reads and writes raw pixels with one decoder-only Transformer - images as patches, video as tubelets, no VAE or vision encoder - and both understands and generates images and video. Weighed against each CharForge stage [E142], its released generation takes text only, at 256 px for an image and 176 x 320 for a video, where the stages need a full-body character at about 1024 px, image-to-image for the face and the character itself in its videos; its understanding scores are those of a 7-8B vision-language model, the level that could not judge moves from words here [E143]; and it runs only on Linux with CUDA. It is not used.

Rigging and skinning

Pinocchio [@pinocchio] embeds a template skeleton in a mesh and computes weights by heat diffusion; RigNet [@rignet] learns joints, connectivity and weights; UniRig [@unirig] predicts skeletons autoregressively with a skeleton-tree tokenisation and was trained on over 14,000 rigged models. Commercial auto-riggers place a humanoid skeleton from guide markers - AccuRIG [@accurig] with correctable markers and finger counts, PINOC [@pinoc] with mirrored markers from chin to ankles, Rig Lab [@riglab] fully automatically, but with each hand a single joint, and Mixamo [@mixamo]. Magnenat-Thalmann et al. describe earlier joint-dependent local deformations for hand animation and grasping [@lbs]. The glTF standard's linear-blend skinning path uses weighted joint transforms [@gltf]; this can collapse bent joints, which dual quaternion skinning [@dqs] can reduce, but not eliminate, by blending rotations as rotations. Our weights follow the idea of geodesic voxel binding [@geodesicvoxel] - distance measured through the voxelised body rather than through the air - so a vest glued to an arm is not near the arm's bone.

Learned rigging has moved on since: Puppeteer [@puppeteer] adds attention-based skinning with topology-aware joint attention and an optimisation that animates the rig from video, and SkinTokens [@skintokens] encodes skinning weights as discrete tokens and predicts skeleton and skin as one sequence, reporting 98-133% better skinning accuracy than earlier methods. Both need an NVIDIA GPU (SkinTokens: 14 GB, CUDA 12.1 and flash-attn), so they are out of reach on this laptop; the geodesic binding we keep runs here, and the extreme-pose battery ([#sec-eval-stress]) now measures it. Make-It-Animatable [@mia] is the learned skinning model that does run here: from a mesh it predicts a Mixamo skeleton, blend weights and a rest-pose reset with a transformer trained on Mixamo characters, and of its CUDA requirements it uses only farthest point sampling and PyTorch3D's transforms, both plain PyTorch - 48 seconds a character on a CPU. Its weights on our skeleton are measured in [#sec-eval-stress] [E157]. AniGen [@anigen] generates shape, skeleton and skin together from one image, the route that would remove the fused surface's rigging problems at the root; a third party's run on an Apple laptop rigged a simple mascot and produced no faces for a clothed character with thin limbs, so it is not tried. For faces, OmniFaceRig [@omnifacerig] registers a template to the character's dense face keypoints, builds the inner mouth procedurally (teeth, gums and tongue, their texture baked) and transfers up to 155 FACS shapes with collision awareness, on an A100 in 20-30 seconds; the template route of [E149] follows the same recipe with the Anny body's 52 ARKit-named facial actions [@anny]. Single-photo face reconstruction has a strong 2026 prior in Pixel3DMM [@pixel3dmm] (per-pixel normals and UV from DINO features constraining a FLAME fit), which needs CUDA and a non-commercial licence.

Expressive hands and faces are often modelled together with the body: SMPL-X [@smplx] extends a body model with articulated hands and an expressive face, while MANO [@mano] learns pose-dependent hand shape corrections from scans. These works motivate testing hand and face deformation as well as landmark placement; their research models are not drop-in templates for arbitrary generated characters. InterHand2.6M [@interhand26m] provides real-captured 3D hand-pose labels, while D3DFACS [@d3dfacs] and the FaMoS sequences released with TEMPEH [@tempeh] provide dynamic human-face/action evidence. Their different topology, style and access terms mean they are calibration references, not direct acceptance tests for stylized meshes [E123] [E124]. AU-Blendshape [@aublendshape] specifically studies stylized action-unit controls and provides a relevant challenge-design reference, but not a CharForge benchmark or a licensed drop-in asset set. Pose-space deformation [@psd] provides a classical basis for the elbow and shoulder correctives planned in [E107].

Layers and motion. CAPE [@cape] learns pose-conditioned clothing shape, and CLOTH3D++ [@cloth3dpp] pairs animated dressed-human sequences with dense cloth geometry and texture. They provide useful challenge cases for layered motion, but neither demonstrates CharForge's runtime spring chains or guarantees stylized garment behavior [E110] [E125].

Motion from video

Human pose models read 2D joints from images: ViTPose [@vitpose] for the body, DWPose [@dwpose] for the whole body including 21 points a hand. Recovering 3D motion from them is usually done by fitting a parametric body such as SMPL [@smpl] - by optimisation, as in SMPLify [@smplify], or by regression, as in HMR, 4D Humans and WHAM [@hmr; @4dhumans; @wham], the last grounded in the world with foot contact. We do not fit a parametric body: the target is the character's own skeleton and mesh, whose proportions (a cartoon's head, an anime character's legs) no human body model shares. Instead we retrieve the nearest clip from the 2,453-animation Mixamo mirror [@mixamomirror] by dynamic time warping [@dtw] of its 2D projections, bend it toward the video, and refine the character's own skinned mesh against the video in the spirit of SMPLify. The videos themselves come from the community Ref2VA fine-tune [@h3] based on MiniMax H3 [@h3base], conditioned on the character's picture; Comfy-Org supplies separately packaged encoder and VAE components [@h3comfy].

Motion ground truth. AMASS [@amass] unifies optical mocap datasets as rigged 3D motion, making it useful for controlled renders with exact motion truth but not paired RGB. TotalCapture [@totalcapture] provides synchronized calibrated video, IMU and Vicon labels; 3DPW [@3dpw] adds in-the-wild footage from a moving phone camera with IMU/video-derived poses. AIST++ [@aistpp] broadens action coverage with multi-view dance footage and reconstructed 3D motion, but its annotations are not Vicon measurements. These sources test different failure modes and label quality, so we keep them as separate conditions rather than pooling them [E111].

Contact labels. UnderPressure [@underpressure] records pressure insoles with motion capture and derives foot-contact labels. It supports evaluating contact classification against an independent signal; the geometric sole-depth threshold and foot-slip ratio in CharForge remain separate proxies and need their own calibration [E122]. Pražák et al. [@footskateperception] found small footsliding perceptible in most tested conditions, but their controlled mannequin study does not establish a universal acceptance threshold for generated characters.

Measuring generated assets and delivery

TIFA [@tifa] and GenEval [@geneval] use fine-grained question or object checks to expose prompt attributes, counts and relations that a holistic image-text score can miss; neither is validated for stylized character renders. MATE-3D [@mate3d] instead collects human ratings for semantic alignment, geometry, texture and overall quality separately. THUman 2.0 [@thuman2] supplies scan meshes and textures for a permission-checked scan-anchored study; rendered orbit views are synthetic views, not captured calibrated camera observations, and redistribution of derived data requires written permission under the posted terms. We use these works to design and calibrate character-specific measurements, not to assume a published image or 3D evaluator transfers unchanged [E102] [E112] [E113].

Metro-style surface distance [@metro] complements the existing nonmanifold checks with source-to-retopo shape error [E118]. Texture Mapping Progressive Meshes [@texmap] motivates checking local UV stretch and anisotropy beyond median texel density [E119]. Unity's mip documentation [@unitymips] describes runtime selection and trilinear blending across downsampled levels; therefore a base-resolution gutter alone does not establish that exported color, normal and ORM maps are seam-free at runtime [E120].

For animated humans, DDH-QA [@ddhqa] includes human-rated binding errors and motion unnaturalness, while DHQA-4D [@dhqa4d] studies geometry, texture and temporal-discontinuity distortions in scanned 4D humans. These taxonomies are useful for designing CharForge's deformation audit, but their learned quality predictors are not assumed to generalize to generated, stylized characters [E102].

The Khronos glTF Validator [@gltfvalidator] checks conformance to glTF 2.0, while Unity, Unreal and Godot document importer-specific behavior for rigs, clips, morphs, materials and skin influences [@unityimport; @unrealimport; @godotimport]. A standards-valid file is therefore necessary but not sufficient evidence of engine-ready behavior; the importer/runtime smoke matrix is [E114].

For traceability, W3C PROV-DM [@provdm] models entities, activities, agents and derivations; CycloneDX's ML-BOM [@cyclonedxmlbom] gives an inventory pattern for models and data, and Sandve et al. [@sandve] emphasize archiving exact computational procedures. They motivate a claim ledger and output-lineage manifest, not a claim of formal provenance compliance [E115] [E121].
