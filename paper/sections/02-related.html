Related work

Characters from an image or a prompt

Image-to-3D generators. TRELLIS [@trellis] and its successor TRELLIS.2 [@trellis2] generate meshes with physically based materials from structured sparse-voxel latents (a 10243 asset in about 9.6K tokens); Hunyuan3D 2.0 and 2.1 [@hunyuan3d2; @hunyuan3d21] pair a shape diffusion transformer with a texture painter and produce PBR materials. Both families are general-purpose: they model a character as one surface, which is the root of most of what CharForge repairs. A practitioner's survey of hosted and open generators [@cinevvaguide] recommends the same two-step workflow we use - draw a clean reference with an image model, then lift it to 3D - and notes the same weakness: generated topology lacks deformation loops at the joints. Hosted services built on these models [@cinevvatool; @ai3dgen] add rigging and preset clips; their free tiers can be as limited as an untextured OBJ at a fixed face count [@ai3dgen].

Character-specific systems. CharacterGen [@charactergen] canonicalises a character in any pose to four A-pose views with an image-conditioned multi-view diffusion model, reconstructs it with a transformer sparse-view model, and back-projects the views into a texture with a depth test and Poisson blending [@poisson]; it rigs with AccuRIG [@accurig]. Make-A-Character [@mach] takes the opposite route: a language model parses the prompt into attributes, a portrait is generated and fitted onto a fixed, production-rigged template (MetaHuman) using 431 dense facial landmarks, hair is built as strands, and garments are matched from a library by CLIP similarity. Make-A-Character 2 [@mach2] starts from a single portrait, corrects its lighting and skin tone, and calibrates a facial skeleton for expressive animation. Template methods get rigging and faces for free but not the look of an arbitrary character; generation methods get the look but inherit the generator's topology. CharForge generates the character (like CharacterGen) and then builds the rig from the generated solid itself.

Characters from drawings. Animated Drawings [@animateddrawings] rigs a child's drawing in 2D. DrawingSpinUp [@drawingspinup] lifts a drawing to 3D, removes and later restores its view-dependent contour lines, thins single-line limbs with a skeleton-guided deformation, rigs with Mixamo's auto-rigger from eight front-view keypoints and assigns each vertex to its nearest bone [@mixamo]; its authors report surface adhesion where the drawing has self-occlusion, the same failure our arm cut addresses. Akman et al. [@akman2020] go from stick figures to 3D bodies with a variational autoencoder and animate by interpolating in its latent space.

Rigging and skinning

Pinocchio [@pinocchio] embeds a template skeleton in a mesh and computes weights by heat diffusion; RigNet [@rignet] learns joints, connectivity and weights; UniRig [@unirig] predicts skeletons autoregressively with a skeleton-tree tokenisation and was trained on over 14,000 rigged models. Commercial auto-riggers place a humanoid skeleton from guide markers - AccuRIG [@accurig] with correctable markers and finger counts, PINOC [@pinoc] with mirrored markers from chin to ankles, Rig Lab [@riglab] fully automatically, but with each hand a single joint. Linear blend skinning [@lbs] is what engines apply; it collapses bent joints, which dual quaternion skinning [@dqs] avoids by blending rotations as rotations. Our weights follow the idea of geodesic voxel binding [@geodesicvoxel] - distance measured through the voxelised body rather than through the air - so a vest glued to an arm is not near the arm's bone.

Motion from video

Human pose models read 2D joints from images: ViTPose [@vitpose] for the body, DWPose [@dwpose] for the whole body including 21 points a hand. Recovering 3D motion from them is usually done by fitting a parametric body such as SMPL [@smpl] - by optimisation, as in SMPLify [@smplify], or by regression, as in HMR, 4D Humans and WHAM [@hmr; @4dhumans; @wham], the last grounded in the world with foot contact. We do not fit a parametric body: the target is the character's own skeleton and mesh, whose proportions (a cartoon's head, an anime character's legs) no human body model shares. Instead we retrieve the nearest clip from a motion-capture library [@mixamo] by dynamic time warping [@dtw] of its 2D projections, bend it toward the video, and refine the character's own skinned mesh against the video in the spirit of SMPLify. The videos themselves come from a reference-to-video model [@h3] conditioned on the character's picture, so the performer already looks like the character.
